# Principle 1 — Coach the Journey

**COACH**

The first principle is: **Coach the Journey.**

When we started, our kids were third graders.

Everything felt hard. Programming was hard. Building a consistent robot was hard. Innovation Project was hard. Just understanding how an FLL season worked was hard.

In particular, coding in the SPIKE App was challenging at first. The students were trying to figure out what all the different blocks did, how to combine them, and how to make the robot do something as basic as drive straight or make a reliable turn.

But every year, the baseline moved.

The coding that had once felt overwhelming became something the students could use confidently. By the end of our last FLL season, they were coding in Python. They had moved from block coding to a text-based programming language, which helped prepare them for the transition to FIRST Tech Challenge.

Mechanisms got more sophisticated. Projects got bigger. The kids got better at interviewing experts, testing ideas, presenting, and working together.

What used to be hard became normal, and then new challenges took its place.

That’s one of the most important things I learned as a coach: don’t judge everything based on one season.

Think about what you’re building that carries forward.

The goal isn’t just to build this year’s robot. It’s to build next year’s team.

I want one of the students to describe what that progression felt like from their side.

**STUDENT 1**

In the early years, coding in the SPIKE App was one of the hardest parts for us. We had to learn what all the blocks did, how to put them together, and how to make the robot drive straight or turn the way we wanted.

At first, even small changes could make the robot behave completely differently. We had to experiment a lot to understand how the code affected the robot.

But after doing FLL for several seasons, coding became much more natural. By our last season, we were writing Python instead of only using blocks. That was a big step because it introduced us to a text-based language and helped us start preparing for FTC.

Every year, something that used to be a challenge became something we just knew how to do.

**COACH**

That’s exactly the idea. Success compounds across seasons.

# Principle 2 — Play the Whole Game

**COACH**

Principle two is: **Play the Whole Game.**

The robot is the big shiny thing. It sits in the middle of the room. Kids want to work on it. Coaches want to work on it. And at competition, it’s often the part parents see most clearly.

So Robot Game can very easily consume the season.

But FLL is much broader than that. You have Robot Design, Innovation Project, Robot Game, and Core Values.

Our team actually spent a tremendous amount of time on Innovation Project. In some seasons, probably more than we spent on Robot Game.

And the kids loved it.

I’ll let one of them tell you why.

**STUDENT 2**

During SUBMERGED, one of the experts we met was Phil Parisi. Phil operated underwater ROVs, and he brought one with him when we met at a dock where we were deploying our experiment.

While we were there, another one of our experts, Jon, accidentally dropped his car keys into the water. They sank about 20 feet.

So Phil pulled out the ROV, and we got to help pilot it down to the bottom and use the gripper to try to recover the keys.

On the camera, we could actually see a crab near the keys, and it looked like the crab was trying to grab one of them before we could.

We eventually got the keys back, but the whole experience was something we never would have had if we had treated Innovation Project like the side activity and spent all of our time on the robot.

**COACH**

That’s exactly why I say **Play the Whole Game**.

Robot Game may be the part everyone sees from the bleachers, but some of the most memorable learning experiences can happen somewhere else.

A balanced season gives more students more ways to contribute, and it makes the experience richer than just chasing points on the table.

# Principle 3 — Start With an Expert

**COACH**

For Innovation Project, one of the best approaches we found was: **Start With an Expert.**

Teams are often told, “Find a problem.”

That sounds simple, but it’s actually a hard thing for kids to do in the abstract.

Instead, we would start by asking: **Who is an expert in this space?**

The team can begin by identifying people who work in or understand the area they are exploring. These might be researchers, engineers, technicians, artists, community leaders, business owners, or people who experience the issue directly.

Then we told them: **use your network.**

Ask your parents. Ask your grandparents. Ask family friends. Ask teachers.

You may not know an expert directly, but someone you know may know one.

Often, one extra hop through your network is enough to connect with someone who has firsthand knowledge.

Once you find an expert, ask them about the problems they actually experience.

Ask questions like: **What is frustrating about your work? What takes too much time? What do you wish worked better? What problem do you encounter repeatedly?**

Those conversations are often the best way to find a problem that is real, specific, and currently affecting someone.

Instead of inventing a problem from a distance, the team can learn directly from a person who experiences it.

One other thing I’d encourage teams to look for is a problem you can **find locally, but that isn’t constrained locally**.

You want something you can actually see, access, test, and understand nearby, but ideally the same problem exists more broadly.

Let’s hear what that process felt like as a student.

**STUDENT 3**

During the SUBMERGED season, we started by brainstorming people who worked in ocean exploration. We thought about underwater divers, ocean engineers, and marine biologists.

We found a diver and an ocean engineer who worked at the same company as one of our parents. Because of that connection, we were able to reach out to them through our network.

They became much more than experts we met with one time. They became collaborators throughout the whole project.

We first met with them on Zoom and learned about biofouling on ocean sensors.

Later, they helped us deploy our experiment in the ocean and gave us a tour of their research vessel.

Having them involved throughout the project helped us understand the problem, improve our experiment, and see how our work connected to real ocean research.

**COACH**

That gives the team something grounded and testable without limiting how broadly the solution might matter.

# Principle 4 — Diversify Your Experts

**COACH**

Once you find experts, the next principle is: **Diversify Your Experts.**

A common instinct is to find one expert, then find four more people who do basically the same thing.

But innovation often comes from combining very different perspectives.

During the MASTERPIECE season, the challenge was to combine art and technology to solve a problem related to a hobby. Many of our team members were interested in hiking, so we started there.

We talked with our local trail master and learned that our area needed more volunteers and community support to develop and maintain hiking trails.

That gave us the problem we wanted to address: how could we use art and technology to draw people in and get them interested in supporting local trail development?

Then we looked for experts who could help us think beyond hiking.

We talked with a cosplay artist who built interactive costumes using lights, sounds, and electronics to control the effects.

We also talked with an artist who created beautiful interactive artwork using sound and light.

Those experts helped us combine ideas from several different worlds: hiking, technology, electronics, sound, light, and visual art.

We used those ideas to create our own interactive, hiking-themed art display. The display was designed to attract people, give them something engaging to experience, and create an opportunity for us to talk with them about supporting local trail development.

The diverse experts helped us turn a local need into an innovative solution that combined our hobby, technology, and art.

I’ll let a student explain why that mattered.

**STUDENT 1**

Talking to different kinds of experts gave us ideas we probably never would have gotten if we had only talked to hikers.

The trail master helped us understand the problem and why more volunteers and support were needed.

The cosplay artist showed us how electronics, lights, and sounds could make something interactive.

The other artist helped us think about how to use light and sound to create an experience that would draw people in.

None of those experts had the complete solution by themselves. The innovation came from combining what each person knew.

**COACH**

So instead of asking, “Who else understands our exact problem?” ask:

**Who understands one piece of our possible solution from a completely different world?**

# Principle 5 — Go Physical

**COACH**

The next principle is: **Go Physical.**

Kids today grow up surrounded by software, so when you ask them to invent something, a common first answer is, “We’ll make an app.”

Sometimes an app is absolutely the right solution.

But for younger students, physical ideas can be incredibly powerful.

A physical prototype gives them something they can touch, change, break, rebuild, test, and explain.

Our SUBMERGED Innovation Project was a great example of this.

Our problem was **biofouling** — biological growth that builds up on ocean-deployed sensors and can interfere with their operation.

We wanted to investigate whether ultraviolet light could prevent or reduce that growth on sensors.

And this project became very, very physical.

We built a test rig out of PVC pipe. We 3D-printed mock sensors. We wired up electrical systems for the ultraviolet lights. We had to waterproof the electronics. We added counter-buoyancy and weights so the whole system would behave the way we wanted in the water.

Then we actually deployed the entire experiment in the ocean.

Not once, but twice.

The kids had to think about construction, electrical systems, waterproofing, buoyancy, deployment, retrieval, and how to make the experiment survive in a real environment.

And then, after three weeks, we pulled this disgusting, stinky biofilm experiment out of the water.

It was gross.

It was also incredibly memorable.

That’s what I mean when I say **Go Physical**.

The students weren’t just talking about an ocean problem. They were building something, putting it in the ocean, waiting, retrieving it, and seeing the results with their own eyes.

There was a huge amount of engineering in that process, and every physical constraint created another opportunity for the kids to learn.

**STUDENT 2**

The project definitely felt real because we actually had to build everything.

We couldn’t just say, “We’ll put some UV lights on a sensor.”

We had to figure out how to mount everything, how to power it, how to keep the electronics dry, how to keep the whole thing from floating away or sinking, and how to get it into the ocean and back out again.

And when we pulled it out after three weeks, it was disgusting. There was this gross biofilm all over the experiment.

But that was also one of the coolest parts, because we had actually done the experiment. We could look at what happened and compare the different parts of the test.

**COACH**

That kind of experience is hard to replicate with a purely digital project.

So when you’re helping a team develop an Innovation Project, don’t be afraid of ideas that require them to build something substantial.

Let them get their hands dirty.

Let them solve real physical problems.

Let them deploy something into the real world and then pull it back out three weeks later smelling absolutely terrible.

Those are the experiences kids remember.

# Principle 6 — Wow the Judges

**COACH**

That leads into the next principle: **Wow the Judges.**

For presentations, I would encourage teams to think less about digital PowerPoint and more about what I call **analog PowerPoint**.

Large-format printed visuals. Cardstock cue cards. Props. Models. Samples. Prototypes. Things judges can hold.

Judging moves quickly, and you want your ideas to be easy to understand and easy to remember.

A physical prop can communicate something in seconds that might otherwise take a minute to explain.

And when you put something into a judge’s hands, you create engagement.

Instead of asking, “What slides should we make?” ask:

**What should the judges see?**

**What should they touch?**

**What can we physically show them that makes the idea obvious?**

The goal isn’t gimmicks. The goal is clarity and memorability.

**STUDENT 3**

Our SUBMERGED project gave us a great example of how putting a prototype in the judges’ hands could make an innovation more memorable.

Our solution for biofouling on ocean sensors was a bendable, shapeable ultraviolet light system.

We created a prototype of the bendable light and 3D-printed several sensor models to demonstrate how it could work.

During the presentation, we put the light in the judge’s hands and asked them to bend it around the different sensors.

That let them feel the flexibility and see how the system could adapt to different sensor shapes, which was the innovative part of our solution.

We could have bent the light around a sensor ourselves and simply shown the judge, but having the judge hold it, feel it, and bend it made a much stronger impression.

**COACH**

And that makes the presentation feel more natural for the students too.

# Principle 7 — Work Backwards

**COACH**

Now moving into robot work, one of my favorite principles is: **Work Backwards.**

When kids see the FLL field, there is a natural tendency to start with the robot.

How are we going to drive over there? How do we program the turn? How do we line up?

I would encourage you to ignore all of that at first.

Start at the mission.

In fact, you don’t even need a complete robot.

Build the mechanism first. Push the chassis up to the mission by hand and operate the mechanism manually.

No motors. No code.

Can it actually complete the mission?

Does it approach from the right direction?

Where does the pivot point need to be?

Does the mechanism need to lift, rotate, slide, or do some combination of those motions?

Solve the manipulation problem first. Navigation is a separate problem.

We had a really good example of this during SUBMERGED.

**STUDENT 1**

The Coral Tree mission was probably the mission we struggled with the most early in the season.

It required this strange combination of twisting and lifting to get the tree into exactly the right position so it would hang from an axle.

Our first approach was to come at it from the south with a mechanism that basically scissored the tree upward.

We spent a lot of time trying to make that work, but it just wasn’t consistent enough.

Later, we changed the whole approach and tried coming from the east with a rotating mechanism.

That created a different problem: we had to find exactly the right rotation point.

If the pivot was just a little too high, low, close, or far away, the tree ended up in the wrong place and wouldn’t hang correctly.

So we kept pushing the chassis up to the mission by hand, changing the mechanism, moving the pivot point, and testing it again.

We could iterate really quickly because we weren’t also worrying about driving or code.

By the end of the season, a mission that had been one of our least reliable early on was working nearly 100% of the time.

**COACH**

That’s why I like working backwards.

If we had tried to solve the driving, alignment, code, and mechanism all at once, every failure would have been harder to diagnose.

Instead, we could isolate the problem.

First: **Can we make the physical interaction work?**

Once that answer was yes, then we could worry about getting the robot there.

**First prove the tool. Then solve the trip.**

# Principle 8 — 2:30s

**COACH**

The next principle is something the Mavericks called **2:30s**.

That simply meant: run the entire Robot Game.

Two minutes and thirty seconds.

Not one mission. Not one launch. Not one attachment.

The whole match.

We made a big deal out of it.

We had a big timer displayed on a TV next to the field.

We didn’t even bother with the LEGO 3-2-1 countdown.

Everybody got ready, the timer started, and the team ran the whole match.

But we didn’t just practice — **we collected data**.

For every 2:30, we tracked whether each mission was a success, partial success, or failure. We also kept track of how long it took us to transition between runs.

After enough matches, patterns started appearing.

I’ll let one of the students explain what we found.

**STUDENT 2**

The chart on this slide came from our 2:30 data.

One thing that really stood out was that our missions later in the match weren’t working as consistently as the missions at the beginning.

**“Send over the submersible”** was especially unreliable.

That was useful because now we knew where to spend our practice time. Instead of just saying, “The robot needs to get better,” we could look at the data and focus on the particular runs and missions that were causing problems.

And the solution wasn’t always changing the code or rebuilding the mechanism.

We were also tracking our transition times between runs.

Sometimes we discovered that the robot was fine — **we were just taking too long to get the next run ready.**

So we worked on how the technicians could work together better: who grabbed the next attachment, who reset the robot, who positioned everything, and how we could get the next run launched faster.

That was something we really only noticed when we practiced the full two minutes and thirty seconds.

**COACH**

That’s an important distinction.

Reliability isn’t just, “Does this program work?”

It’s: **Does the entire team system work for two minutes and thirty seconds?**

The robot, the code, the mechanisms, the technicians, the attachment changes, the launches, the recovery decisions — all of it.

And once you collect enough data, you stop guessing about what needs improvement.

The patterns tell you.

Our mindset became:

**You don’t compete one mission at a time, so don’t only practice one mission at a time.**

# Principle 9 — Parent Showcase

**COACH**

The next principle is **Parent Showcase**.

One thing I noticed at FLL competitions is that parents often don’t really get to see the Robot Game.

They’re there. They’re cheering. But they may be in bleachers or behind an audience line quite a distance from the table.

They see the robot moving around, but they don’t necessarily see the mechanisms, the alignment techniques, the quick attachment changes, or all the small things the kids have spent months developing.

So we liked having a Parent Showcase.

Bring the families in. Put them right next to the field.

Have the kids run a full 2:30 match with parents standing just a few feet away.

Now the parents can actually appreciate what their kids built and just how much is happening during those two and a half minutes.

I’ll let one of the students share what that changed for one of our families.

**STUDENT 3**

One of our parents, Mrs. Zink, used to describe the team as a LEGO club.

From her perspective, Bianca was going to meetings and building things with LEGO.

Then she came to one of our Parent Showcases and watched the Robot Game from just a few feet away.

She could see the robot launch, the mechanisms operate, the quick attachment changes, the timing, and how much the team had to coordinate during the match.

After that, she stopped telling people Bianca was in a LEGO club.

She started telling people Bianca was on a robotics team.

**COACH**

That shift in language told me the showcase had done exactly what we wanted it to do.

It helped parents see the engineering, programming, teamwork, and preparation that can be hard to appreciate from the bleachers.

# Principle 10 — Mock Judging

**COACH**

The last principle is **Mock Judging**.

The important thing here is to practice the entire judging workflow — not just the presentation.

Do the Innovation Project presentation. Then Q&A. Then Robot Design. Then Q&A.

Go through the entire experience.

Parents are great for this because they can act as judges, but give them a question bank and encourage them to ask the difficult questions.

That way, the quality of the practice doesn’t depend on whether a particular parent knows robotics or Innovation Project well.

And there is one important rule:

**Don’t coach during the mock judging.**

The parents are judges until the session is finished.

Let the kids struggle with a question. Let them figure out who is going to answer. Let there be an awkward pause.

That’s useful practice.

Then, after the entire session, switch roles.

Now the parents can give constructive feedback.

What was clear?

What was confusing?

Who wasn’t speaking?

Which answers were too long?

What important thing did the team forget to mention?

One of our mock judging sessions showed us exactly why this was valuable.

**STUDENT 1**

One of the parents somehow figured out the exact question we really didn’t want the judges to ask.

During SUBMERGED, we deployed our Innovation Project experiment underwater to test ultraviolet light as a way to prevent biofouling on ocean sensors.

But part of the experiment failed.

Water leaked into the system and our ultraviolet light stopped working.

So during mock judging, one of the parents basically asked us:

**“If your experiment failed, how do you know your solution actually works?”**

That was a really hard question.

But because they asked it before competition, we had time to think about what had actually happened and how to explain it.

We realized that the failure itself had taught us a lot.

We went back to experts and learned much more about underwater waterproofing.

We even learned about a company that had around 15 Ph.D.s on staff focused specifically on waterproofing problems.

That helped us understand that waterproofing wasn’t some simple mistake we should have magically known how to solve. It was a serious engineering challenge.

So instead of trying to hide the failure, we learned how to explain what failed, what we learned from it, and what we would change next.

After practicing a question that difficult, the questions we got from the actual judges felt easy.

**COACH**

That’s what I want from mock judging.

Don’t prepare the students to answer the questions you hope they get.

Prepare them for the questions you hope they **don’t** get.

If the parents can make the practice room harder than the judging room, the students walk into competition confident and overprepared.

# Closing Story — The Last Match

**COACH**

I want to finish the presentation part with one story from the Mavericks’ final season.

By that point, we had spent five years building toward that season.

These kids had done hundreds of robot runs. They had worked through multiple Innovation Projects. They had learned how to code, how to design, how to present, how to interview experts, and how to work together under pressure.

Then we got to the FIRST Championship in Houston.

And in our very first Robot Game match, we had a motor failure.

After all that preparation, that is how Worlds started.

But something else we had built over those years mattered in that moment too: relationships.

We had become friends with a team from Spain, and they loaned us a replacement motor.

The kids had to repair the robot quickly, test it, and get themselves ready to compete again.

I want one of the students to take the story from there.

**STUDENT 2**

That first match was stressful because it felt like the worst possible time for something to break.

But we didn’t really have time to sit around and be upset about it.

We had to figure out what failed, get the replacement motor, repair the robot, and make sure it was ready for the next match.

By the time we got to our final Robot Game match, it was also the final match we would ever do in FLL.

And somehow, that ended up being the best run we had ever had — better than any practice run and better than any previous competition run.

**COACH**

Their very last Robot Game match.

Then came the awards ceremony.

These kids had spent five years growing together, and they really hoped they might finish their FLL journey with an award.

The ceremony kept moving until there were only two names left to call: Champions Finalist and Champion.

For a moment, we thought we had missed it.

**STUDENT 3**

I remember thinking that our chance was probably gone.

Then they announced the Intelligent Mavericks as Champions Finalist.

It was one of those moments where it took a second to realize they had actually said our name.

**COACH**

It was an incredible moment.

But for me, that moment was not really about the trophy.

It was five years of small things adding up.

Every failed robot run.

Every expert interview.

Every prototype.

Every 2:30.

Every presentation.

Every mistake.

Every hard thing that eventually became easy.

Even that first-match motor failure mattered, because they had learned how to recover.

They had built relationships with other teams.

They had practiced handling things that didn’t go according to plan.

That is why my first principle today was **Coach the Journey**.

You never really know which practice, which failure, which relationship, or which lesson is going to matter later.

So don’t just coach the season.

**Coach the journey.**

# Principles to Take Home

**COACH**

So those are the ten principles we wanted to share.

1. **Coach the Journey**
2. **Play the Whole Game**
3. **Start With an Expert**
4. **Diversify Your Experts**
5. **Go Physical**
6. **Wow the Judges**
7. **Work Backwards**
8. **2:30s**
9. **Parent Showcase**
10. **Mock Judging**

You absolutely do not need to adopt all ten.

If you heard two or three ideas today that make you think, “We could try that with our team,” that’s enough.

The bigger message is to build habits and then let those habits compound.

A team does not have to be sophisticated in its first season.

Every year, the students learn.

Every year, the coaches learn.

And every season gives you a new foundation to build on.

# Coaches Connect — Full Presentation Script

**Supporting speakers:** 2–3 former Intelligent Mavericks students

> **Speaker key**
>
> - **COACH** — Primary host and presenter

# Principle 1 — Coach the Journey

**COACH:** The first principle is: **Coach the Journey.**

**COACH:** When we started, our kids were third graders.

**COACH:** Everything felt hard. Programming was hard. Building a consistent robot was hard. Innovation Project was hard. Just understanding how an FLL season worked was hard.

**COACH:** But every year, the baseline moved.

**COACH:** Programming that had once taken a lot of effort became routine. Mechanisms got more sophisticated. Projects got bigger. The kids got better at interviewing experts, testing ideas, presenting, and working together.

**COACH:** What used to be hard became normal, and then new challenges took its place.

**COACH:** That’s one of the most important things I learned as a coach: don’t judge everything based on one season.

**COACH:** Think about what you’re building that carries forward.

**COACH:** The goal isn’t just to build this year’s robot. It’s to build next year’s team.

**COACH:** I want one of the students to describe what that progression felt like from their side.

**STUDENT 1:** In the early years, there were things that felt really advanced to us. Even getting the robot to drive consistently or making a mechanism work could take a long time.

**STUDENT 1:** But after doing FLL for several seasons, those things stopped being the scary part. We could start from a much higher level, so we had more time to work on harder mechanisms, better code, more testing, and bigger project ideas.

**STUDENT 1:** Every year, something that used to be a challenge became something we just knew how to do.

**COACH:** That’s exactly the idea. Success compounds across seasons.

---

# Principle 2 — Play the Whole Game

**COACH:** Principle two is: **Play the Whole Game.**

**COACH:** The robot is the big shiny thing. It sits in the middle of the room. Kids want to work on it. Coaches want to work on it. Everyone can see whether it succeeds or fails.

**COACH:** So Robot Game can very easily consume the season.

**COACH:** But FLL is much broader than that. You have Robot Design, Innovation Project, Robot Game, and Core Values.

**COACH:** Our team actually spent a tremendous amount of time on Innovation Project. In some seasons, probably more than we spent on Robot Game.

**COACH:** And the kids loved it.

**COACH:** I’ll let one of them speak to that.

**STUDENT 2:** Innovation Project became one of our favorite parts of FLL.

**STUDENT 2:** We got to talk to real people, go places, build things that weren’t just LEGO robots, and keep changing our idea as we learned more.

**STUDENT 2:** Now that some of us are in FTC, that’s actually one of the things we miss most, because FTC doesn’t have the same kind of Innovation Project.

**COACH:** So I would encourage coaches to intentionally protect time for every part of the program.

**COACH:** A balanced season gives more students more ways to contribute, and it makes the experience richer than just chasing points on the table.

---

# Principle 3 — Start With a Person

**COACH:** For Innovation Project, one of the best approaches we found was: **Start With a Person.**

**COACH:** Teams are often told, “Find a problem.”

**COACH:** That sounds simple, but it’s actually a hard thing for kids to do in the abstract.

**COACH:** Instead, we would start by asking: **Who works in this space?**

**COACH:** During SUBMERGED, for example, the theme involved ocean exploration.

**COACH:** So instead of immediately asking, “What ocean problem should we solve?” we asked the kids to identify job roles.

**COACH:** Divers. Marine biologists. Ocean researchers. People who work on boats. Engineers.

**COACH:** Then we told them: **use your network.**

**COACH:** Ask your parents. Ask your grandparents. Ask family friends. Ask teachers.

**COACH:** You may not know a marine biologist, but someone you know may know one.

**COACH:** Often, one extra hop through your network is enough to get to a real person.

**COACH:** Once you find that person, you can ask about real problems they actually experience.

**COACH:** One other thing I’d encourage teams to look for is a problem you can **find locally, but that isn’t constrained locally**.

**COACH:** You want something you can actually see, access, test, and understand nearby, but ideally the same problem exists more broadly.

**COACH:** Let’s hear what that process felt like as a student.

**STUDENT 3:** Starting with a person made the project feel much more real.

**STUDENT 3:** Instead of sitting around trying to invent some giant problem, we could ask someone, “What is actually frustrating about your job?” or “What do you wish worked better?”

**STUDENT 3:** And once we found something local, we could ask whether people in other places had the same problem too.

**COACH:** That gives the team something grounded and testable without limiting how broadly the solution might matter.

---

# Principle 4 — Diversify Your Experts

**COACH:** Once you find experts, the next principle is: **Diversify Your Experts.**

**COACH:** A common instinct is to find one expert, then find four more people who do basically the same thing.

**COACH:** But innovation often comes from combining very different perspectives.

**COACH:** During MASTERPIECE, our project was connected to hiking.

**COACH:** So yes, we worked with trail experts from Friends of Badger.

**COACH:** But we also worked with Cheyanne, who had expertise in cosplay and costume design, and Andy Arkley, who is an artist.

**COACH:** Those people came at the problem from completely different directions.

**COACH:** A trail expert understands the environment. A costume expert may think about materials, wearability, comfort, movement, and fabrication. An artist may think about form, communication, interaction, and experience.

**COACH:** I’ll let a student explain why that mattered.

**STUDENT 1:** Talking to different kinds of experts gave us ideas we probably never would have gotten if we had only talked to hikers.

**STUDENT 1:** Each person noticed something different. Sometimes the most useful suggestion came from someone who wasn’t an expert in our exact problem at all.

**COACH:** So instead of asking, “Who else understands our exact problem?” ask: **Who understands one piece of our possible solution from a completely different world?**

---

# Principle 5 — Go Physical

**COACH:** The next principle is: **Go Physical.**

**COACH:** Kids today grow up surrounded by software, so when you ask them to invent something, a common first answer is, “We’ll make an app.”

**COACH:** Sometimes an app is absolutely the right solution.

**COACH:** But for younger students, physical ideas can be incredibly powerful.

**COACH:** A physical prototype gives them something they can touch, change, break, rebuild, test, and explain.

**COACH:** Lucy once made a connection to two books we used to read when the kids were younger: *If I Built a Car* and *If I Built a House*.

**COACH:** The ideas in those books are crazy. They’re fun. They’re weird. They aren’t constrained by what already exists.

**COACH:** That is a really useful mindset for Innovation Project.

**COACH:** Give kids permission to imagine something physical and a little bit strange, and then build some version of it.

**COACH:** Cardboard. LEGO. Foam board. Fabric. Tape. 3D printing. Whatever makes the idea real.

**COACH:** Lucy, since this was your connection, tell them what you meant by that.

**STUDENT 2:** What I liked about those books is that they didn’t start by asking whether the idea was practical.

**STUDENT 2:** They started with, “Wouldn’t it be cool if...?”

**STUDENT 2:** That makes it easier to come up with something original. Then later you can figure out which parts are realistic and build a prototype.

**COACH:** Exactly. Let imagination come first, then engineering can make it real.

---

# Principle 6 — Wow the Judges

**COACH:** That leads into the next principle: **Wow the Judges.**

**COACH:** For presentations, I would encourage teams to think less about digital PowerPoint and more about what I call **analog PowerPoint**.

**COACH:** Large-format printed visuals. Cardstock cue cards. Props. Models. Samples. Prototypes. Things judges can hold.

**COACH:** Judging moves quickly, and you want your ideas to be easy to understand and easy to remember.

**COACH:** A physical prop can communicate something in seconds that might otherwise take a minute to explain.

**COACH:** And when you put something into a judge’s hands, you create engagement.

**COACH:** Instead of asking, “What slides should we make?” ask: **What should the judges see? What should they touch? What can we physically show them that makes the idea obvious?**

**COACH:** The goal isn’t gimmicks. The goal is clarity and memorability.

**STUDENT 3:** As students, the props also helped us present.

**STUDENT 3:** If we could point to something, hold it up, or hand it to a judge, it was easier to explain what we meant than trying to remember a long description.

**COACH:** And that makes the presentation feel more natural for the students too.

---

# Principle 7 — Work Backwards

**COACH:** Now moving into robot work, one of my favorite principles is: **Work Backwards.**

**COACH:** When kids see the FLL field, there is a natural tendency to start with the robot.

**COACH:** How are we going to drive over there? How do we program the turn? How do we line up?

**COACH:** I would encourage you to ignore all of that at first.

**COACH:** Start at the mission.

**COACH:** In fact, you don’t even need a robot.

**COACH:** Build the mechanism by itself.

**COACH:** We would sometimes put a mechanism on what we called a sled — something approximately robot-sized that could just be pushed around by hand.

**COACH:** No motors. No code.

**COACH:** Slide it up to the mission. Operate it manually.

**COACH:** Does it actually work? Can it engage the mission reliably? Can you add an aligner? Do you need rotational motion or linear motion? Where should the pivot point be? How high does something need to lift? Would a different gear ratio eventually help? Can one mechanism handle multiple missions?

**COACH:** That is the manipulation problem.

**COACH:** Solve that first. Navigation is a separate problem.

**STUDENT 1:** The sled was useful because we could change things really fast.

**STUDENT 1:** If a mechanism didn’t reach far enough or hit the mission at the wrong angle, we could fix that without also wondering whether the driving code was wrong.

**STUDENT 1:** Once we knew the mechanism worked by hand, then it made sense to put it on the robot.

**COACH:** Exactly. First prove the tool. Then solve the trip.

---

# Principle 8 — 2:30s

**COACH:** The next principle is something the Mavericks called **2:30s**.

**COACH:** That simply meant: run the entire Robot Game.

**COACH:** Two minutes and thirty seconds.

**COACH:** Not one mission. Not one launch. Not one attachment. The whole match.

**COACH:** We made a big deal out of it.

**COACH:** We had a big timer displayed on a TV next to the field.

**COACH:** We didn’t even bother with the LEGO 3-2-1 countdown.

**COACH:** Everybody got ready, the timer started, and the team ran the whole match.

**COACH:** Then we tracked what happened: success, partial success, or failure.

**COACH:** Once you do enough 2:30s, patterns start to emerge.

**COACH:** You discover things you simply don’t see when testing runs independently.

**COACH:** Maybe a mechanism works great, but the attachment change takes too long. Maybe a launch works when someone carefully aligns the robot, but falls apart when they’re rushed. Maybe when something goes wrong, the kids lose twenty seconds trying to rescue it instead of moving on.

**COACH:** Robot Game is not just a software system. It’s a human system too.

**STUDENT 2:** The 2:30s felt much more like competition than normal testing.

**STUDENT 2:** You had to think about what came next, how fast you were changing attachments, whether you should retry something, and how much time was left.

**STUDENT 2:** We definitely found problems in full matches that we never noticed when we ran each mission separately.

**COACH:** Our mindset became: **You don’t compete one mission at a time, so don’t only practice one mission at a time.**

---

# Principle 9 — Parent Showcase

**COACH:** The next principle is **Parent Showcase**.

**COACH:** One thing I noticed at FLL competitions is that parents often don’t really get to see the Robot Game.

**COACH:** They’re there. They’re cheering. But they may be in bleachers or behind an audience line quite a distance from the table.

**COACH:** They see the robot moving around, but they don’t necessarily see the mechanisms, the alignment techniques, the quick attachment changes, or all the small things the kids have spent months developing.

**COACH:** So we liked having a Parent Showcase.

**COACH:** Bring the families in. Put them right next to the field.

**COACH:** Have the kids run a full 2:30 match with parents standing just a few feet away.

**COACH:** Now the parents can actually appreciate what their kids built and just how much is happening during those two and a half minutes.

**STUDENT 3:** It was fun for us too because our parents finally got to see the details.

**STUDENT 3:** At a tournament, they might know we scored points, but at the showcase they could actually see how an attachment worked or how quickly we had to set up the next run.

**COACH:** It gives the students a chance to proudly demonstrate what they’ve accomplished before competition day.

---

# Principle 10 — Mock Judging

**COACH:** The last principle is **Mock Judging**.

**COACH:** The important thing here is to practice the entire judging workflow.

**COACH:** Not just the presentation.

**COACH:** Do the Innovation Project presentation. Then Q&A. Then Robot Design. Then Q&A.

**COACH:** Go through the entire experience.

**COACH:** Parents are great for this because they can act as judges, but I would give them a question bank.

**COACH:** That way, the quality of the practice doesn’t depend on whether a particular parent knows robotics or Innovation Project well.

**COACH:** They can simply choose realistic questions from the bank.

**COACH:** And there is one important rule: **Don’t coach during the mock judging.**

**COACH:** The parents are judges until the session is finished.

**COACH:** Let the kids struggle with a question. Let them figure out who is going to answer. Let there be an awkward pause. That’s useful practice.

**COACH:** Then, after the full judging session, switch roles.

**COACH:** Now the parents can give constructive feedback.

**COACH:** What was clear? What was confusing? Who wasn’t speaking? Which answers were too long? What important thing did the team forget to mention?

**STUDENT 1:** The Q&A was probably the most useful part because you couldn’t completely script it.

**STUDENT 1:** You had to actually understand what the team had done and be ready to explain it in your own words.

**STUDENT 1:** Doing that with parents first made the real judging room feel much more normal.

**COACH:** That’s the goal. Practice the flow, not just the script.

# Closing Story — The Last Match

**COACH:** I want to finish the presentation part with one story from the Mavericks’ final season.

**COACH:** By that point, we had spent five years building toward that season.

**COACH:** These kids had done hundreds of robot runs. They had worked through multiple Innovation Projects. They had learned how to code, how to design, how to present, how to interview experts, and how to work together under pressure.

**COACH:** Then we got to the FIRST Championship in Houston.

**COACH:** And in our very first Robot Game match, we had a motor failure.

**COACH:** After all that preparation, that is how Worlds started.

**COACH:** But something else we had built over those years mattered in that moment too: relationships.

**COACH:** We had become friends with a team from Spain, and they loaned us a replacement motor.

**COACH:** The kids had to repair the robot quickly, test it, and get themselves ready to compete again.

**COACH:** I want one of the students to take the story from there.

**STUDENT 2:** That first match was stressful because it felt like the worst possible time for something to break.

**STUDENT 2:** But we didn’t really have time to sit around and be upset about it. We had to figure out what failed, get the replacement motor, repair the robot, and make sure it was ready for the next match.

**STUDENT 2:** By the time we got to our final Robot Game match, it was also the final match we would ever do in FLL.

**STUDENT 2:** And somehow, that ended up being the best run we had ever had — better than any practice run and better than any previous competition run.

**COACH:** Their very last Robot Game match.

**COACH:** Then came the awards ceremony.

**COACH:** These kids had spent five years growing together, and they really hoped they might finish their FLL journey with an award.

**COACH:** The ceremony kept moving until there were only two names left to call: Champions Finalist and Champion.

**COACH:** For a moment, we thought we had missed it.

**STUDENT 3:** I remember thinking that our chance was probably gone.

**STUDENT 3:** Then they announced the Intelligent Mavericks as Champions Finalist.

**STUDENT 3:** It was one of those moments where it took a second to realize they had actually said our name.

**COACH:** It was an incredible moment.

**COACH:** But for me, that moment was not really about the trophy.

**COACH:** It was five years of small things adding up.

**COACH:** Every failed robot run. Every expert interview. Every prototype. Every 2:30. Every presentation. Every mistake. Every hard thing that eventually became easy.

**COACH:** Even that first-match motor failure mattered, because they had learned how to recover.

**COACH:** They had built relationships with other teams.

**COACH:** They had practiced handling things that didn’t go according to plan.

**COACH:** That is why my first principle today was **Coach the Journey**.

**COACH:** You never really know which practice, which failure, which relationship, or which lesson is going to matter later.

**COACH:** So don’t just coach the season.

**COACH:** **Coach the journey.**
